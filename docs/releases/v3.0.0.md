# v3.0.0 — Read it, or listen to it

Sometimes you want to read the book. Sometimes you want to listen to it —
walking, cooking, driving, or with your eyes shut. Readr does both now, from
the same place in the same book: press Listen and it reads from the top of the
page you're looking at, turning pages to follow the voice, so putting the phone
down and picking it back up doesn't cost you your place either way.

The voice is Apple's, running on your device. That matters beyond the sound of
it: listening is the one AI-adjacent feature that involves no provider, no
account and no key, because nothing about the book leaves the device to be read
aloud. Every cloud text-to-speech service works by uploading the text — Readr's
doesn't upload anything, so the same zero-egress promise reading and local-model
answers already make now covers listening too.

A major version because it's a second way to read a book, not a refinement of
the first.

## What to test

**Listen from where you are.** Open a book, page into the middle of a chapter,
press Listen (⇧⌘L, or the headphones control). It should start at the first
sentence of the page in front of you — never mid-sentence, and never back at the
chapter's start. Let it run past the bottom of the page: the page should turn
itself, in step with the voice.

**Change speed while it's talking.** Open the speed menu mid-sentence and pick a
different one. It should keep going from roughly the word it had reached — not
restart the sentence, and not skip to the next one. Same for switching voice.

**Skip.** The ⏪ and ⏩ controls move one sentence; ⏮ and ⏭ move a chapter. Skip
back at the very start of a book and it restarts the sentence rather than doing
nothing. Skip forward at the very end and it stops; skip to "next chapter" from
the last chapter and it just keeps reading, the way a track control behaves at
the end of a record.

**Sleep.** Set a 5-minute timer and listen. Then pause it for a while and come
back: the countdown should have waited for you — the timer measures listening,
not wall-clock. When it fires, narration stops where it is, and play picks up
that same sentence. "End of chapter" should stop at the chapter wall instead of
reading on.

**Ask while listening.** Open the Ask panel mid-narration. The voice should keep
going — asking the book about the passage it just read to you is the point.

**Lock the phone** (iOS). Playback should continue with the screen off, the
lock screen should show the book with working play/pause, and a headphone pinch
should pause it.

**Listen to a PDF.** The Listen control reads a PDF's extracted text, so it
works on the original-pages view too.

## New

- **Listen to any book, from the page you're on.** A Listen control in the
  reader toolbar (⇧⌘L) and a bar under the page: play/pause, sentence and
  chapter skip, speed, voice, and a sleep timer. The bar insets the page rather
  than floating over it, because the page keeps turning underneath it and
  covering the words being read would defeat the point.

- **The page follows the voice**, and the position Readr saves follows it too.
  Close the book after listening and Continue Reading resumes where you
  *listened* to, not where you last looked.

- **Speed and voice change without losing your place.** Both apply from the word
  the voice had reached, so a mid-sentence change is heard immediately and
  nothing is read twice.

- **A sleep timer that counts listening, not clock time.** 5 to 60 minutes, or
  "end of chapter". Pausing holds the countdown; when it fires, narration keeps
  the sentence so you can pick it back up.

- **Background and lock-screen playback** on iOS: `.spokenAudio` session, Now
  Playing showing the book and author, and remote commands wired to play/pause
  and chapter skip.

- **The voice matches the book, not the device.** A book that declares its
  language gets a voice that can read it. Quality leads — a downloaded enhanced
  voice beats a compact one — and within a tier the platform's own default for
  that language wins, because Apple already ships an answer to "which of these
  is the sensible voice" and it is a better one than any rule of ours.

## Fixed

Everything here was found by CI, by review, or by a verification run on real
hardware *before* the feature shipped. They're recorded because each was a real
defect in the merged branch — and the last three are the ones only a machine
that could actually make a sound was ever going to find.

- **The sleep timer kept counting when narration stopped for reasons other than
  you pausing** — at a chapter wall with auto-advance off, or after an utterance
  the engine refused. The countdown burned while nothing was being read, so the
  first tick after pressing play cut you off. Holding the timer now lives in one
  place every path into a non-speaking state goes through, rather than in each
  caller that had to remember it.

- **A book with no declared language offered every voice on the device.** Most
  PDFs and plenty of EPUBs record no language, and the picker answered that with
  every installed voice in every language — well past a hundred rows on a stock
  iPhone. It falls back to your own locale now.

- **Skipping to "next chapter" from the last chapter declared the book
  finished**, stranding narration mid-chapter behind a play button that then did
  nothing.

- **"No." ended up inside the previous sentence.** `No.`, `Co.`, `Ed.` and
  `Vol.` are abbreviations *and* ordinary words, and were treated as
  abbreviations unconditionally — so "Did he agree? No. He refused." was one
  utterance. Titles like `Mr.` still always carry on, since nothing about what
  follows could tell us otherwise; the rest let the following word decide, and a
  number after them ("No. 5", "Fig. 3") still keeps them joined.

- **Following the voice cleared your text selection every sentence**, which
  silently disabled the annotation shortcuts for the whole listening session,
  and re-armed the debounced position save at sentence rate — rewriting the
  library file every few seconds for as long as anyone listened. Narration's
  page turns are distinguished from yours now.

- **Opening a sheet could stop narration.** Asking the book a question about the
  passage it just read would cut the voice off.

- **An English book was read aloud by "Albert."** On macOS every installed
  English voice reports the same quality tier, so the alphabetical tie-break
  decided everything — and macOS ships novelty voices (Albert, Bad News, Bahh,
  Bells, Boing, Bubbles, Cellos) that sort ahead of every real one. The picker
  opened on them too. Compounding it, `Locale.current.identifier` can return
  `en_US@rg=inzzzz` when a region override is set, and that extension matched no
  voice's locale. Both fixed: the platform's default breaks ties above the
  alphabet, and locale tags drop their extensions before matching.

- **Every speed above 1× was roughly a double lie.** Measured by rendering the
  synthesizer's own audio and timing a sentence: "1.5×" played at 2.49×, "2×" at
  4.04×. AVFoundation's rate scale climbs steeply above its default and the
  mapping crossed it linearly. The fast half is inverted through the measured
  curve now; the slow half measured accurate and is unchanged.

- **Narration hung at the end of a book**, showing Pause with the last sentence
  for minutes: the engine's completion callback never arrived, and every move
  the controller makes is driven by it. There's a backstop now — an engine
  reporting idle while narration believes it is speaking ends the utterance —
  and the engine reports the synthesizer's real state rather than bookkeeping
  the missing callback was supposed to update.

## Under the hood

- A new `ReadrKit.Speech` module, entirely platform-agnostic so it builds and is
  tested on Linux CI: `SpeechSegmenter` (sentence segmentation over character
  offsets into `Chapter.text` — the same coordinates highlights use),
  `SpeechPlaylist` (a cursor across chapters, skipping `linear="no"` documents
  the way the reader's own chapter moves do), `SpeechSettings`, `VoiceSelector`,
  `SleepTimerState`, and `NarrationController` over a `SpeechEngine` protocol.

- **Every playback rule is a unit test against a mock engine** — about 110 of
  them. That includes the ones no device could show you without listening for
  minutes at a time: that a cancelled utterance's completion callback must not
  advance the book, that a speed change resumes at the last word boundary, that
  pausing doesn't spend the sleep timer.

- `AVSpeechEngine` is the only part that touches AVFoundation, and it is glue:
  voice selection, the reader's speed multiplier mapped onto AVFoundation's rate
  scale, and boundary callbacks converted from UTF-16 to the character offsets
  ReadrKit addresses text with. A neural on-device voice can replace it behind
  the same protocol without the controller noticing.

- The XCUITests launch with `-uiTestSilentNarration`, which swaps in a soundless
  engine. The whole pipeline is real; only the sound is absent. Against a live
  synthesizer a headless runner fails the first utterance instantly and races
  the book to its end before a test can tap anything — so the tests would have
  been measuring the runner's audio hardware, not the feature.

  The consequence is worth stating plainly: **CI cannot prove Readr makes a
  sound.** That is what the J8 manual gate in
  [DEVELOPMENT-PLAN.md](../DEVELOPMENT-PLAN.md) is for.

- `UIBackgroundModes: audio` in the generated Info.plist, and a privacy-audit
  test asserting the narration pipeline takes no `HTTPClient` — the same
  structural argument the retrieval pipeline makes.

## Known issue

On at least one Mac, pressing Listen after paging to the last page of a chapter
started narration near the chapter's top instead. Reproduced twice on that
machine and not explained by the code — every route that turns a page commits
the anchor narration starts from — so rather than guess at a fix, the press now
records the chapter and offset it actually handed over. If you hit this, a bug
report (Settings → Help) will carry the number that pins it.
