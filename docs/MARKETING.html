Readr — Marketing & Website Concept

Synthesized from thought dump, 2026-07-23. Iterate here.

Live draft: website artifact · Claude Design project: "Readr Website" · source: docs/marketing/website-mockup.html

1 · The core narrative

The insight that resolves the "rabbitholing vs. distraction-free" tension: rabbitholes are not the enemy of deep reading — browsers are. Today, a question mid-chapter means opening a tab, and forty minutes later you're on YouTube and the book is closed. Readr's promise is that the rabbithole happens inside the book: select, ask, get an answer in the margin, keep reading.

Chase every rabbithole without ever leaving the page.

Everything hangs off two pillars:

  1. Be curious — ask the book anything, stay in the book.
  2. Make it yours — highlight, add your thoughts, walk away with an article in your own voice.

Tagline candidates

  • For the love of rabbitholing.
  • Stay in the book.
  • Read curious.
  • The rabbithole, without leaving the page.
  • Serious reading, curious questions.

My lean: "For the love of rabbitholing." as the emotional hero line, with "Chase every rabbithole without leaving the page." as the explanatory subhead.

Audience

People who love reading — not only the capital-S serious reader. That includes the historian-at-heart and the literary annotator, but equally the person 400 pages into a fantasy doorstopper, the memoir devourer, the one who reads on the train and wishes they annotated. The unifying trait is love of the book, not seriousness about it; the brand should welcome, not gatekeep. Notably: much of this audience still skews AI-skeptical. The brand should lead with reading and let AI be the quiet capability underneath — but named plainly where it explains the difference (see Section 6, decision 3): the hero and trust strip say "AI" outright, because the AI is how Readr differs from every other reader. Quiet everywhere else; the word "AI" should still appear far less often than the word "book."

2 · Pillar 1 — Be curious (ask the book)

The examples are the marketing. Each one should make a specific reader think "that's me." Rotate these on the website; use them in screenshots and demos.

Example bank — diverse books × diverse questions

The Remains of the Day · Kazuo Ishiguro
"What was life actually like for the staff of an English great house in the 1950s? Is this accurate?"
A Brief History of Intelligence · Max Bennett
"How has the neuroscience moved on since this was written?"
This Time Is Different · Reinhart & Rogoff
"Did this hold up after 2008?"
A Game of Thrones · George R. R. Martin, 400 pages in
"Who is this character again? No spoilers past where I am."
The English Patient · Michael Ondaatje
"Who directed the film adaptation? Is it worth watching?"
Anna Karenina · Tolstoy, in translation
"What is this phrase losing from the original Russian?"
Moby-Dick · Herman Melville
"What does this whaling jargon actually mean?"
The Three-Body Problem · Liu Cixin
"Is the sophon physics real, or invented for the plot?"
Long Walk to Freedom · Nelson Mandela
"What happened in the years after the book ends?"
Wolf Hall · Hilary Mantel — the universal one, belongs in the hero demo
"Is this true? Did Cromwell really do this?"
The Song of Achilles · Madeline Miller
"How much of this is actually in the Iliad?"
The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo · Taylor Jenkins Reid
"Is the film adaptation out yet — who's making it?"
Project Hail Mary · Andy Weir
"Could astrophage really exist?"
Fourth Wing · Rebecca Yarros
"Remind me how signets work — only what I've read so far."

Mix the canon with the current: the bank should always pair classics (Moby-Dick, Anna Karenina) with what people are reading right now (Miller, Reid, Weir, Yarros) so both the annotator and the BookTok reader see themselves.

✅ Decided (2026-07-23): answers are book-contextual, not book-limited — the model always has the book as context but may bring world knowledge ("science since then," "film adaptation"), ideally labeling which is which.

⚠️ Implementation gap: the current system prompt (Sources/ReadrKit/Context/ContextStrategy.swift:144) says "Answer the reader's question using the provided book context … say so if the answer is not in the book", and J4's spec in docs/USER-JOURNEYS.md encodes the same book-only behavior. Both need updating to match the decided intent — tracked in issue #54.

"No spoilers" is a sleeper feature for marketing. "Answers only know the book up to where you are" is instantly legible to every fiction reader and no browser can offer it. If the product doesn't do position-aware context yet, flag it as roadmap before we put it on the site.

3 · Pillar 2 — Make it yours (highlights → article)

The journey: highlight → add your thought in the margin → Readr composes your highlights and notes into an editable article → share it, post it, or just keep it.

Example seeds — marginal notes that become articles

The Road · Cormac McCarthy
"I think it's cool the way he depicts human suffering — it's never ornamental"

→ an essay on McCarthy's treatment of suffering, built from your six highlights on it.

The Code Breaker · Walter Isaacson
"What if we could build CRISPR for embryos?"

→ your speculative note plus the passages that provoked it, composed into a shareable piece.

Sapiens · Yuval Noah Harari
"This contradicts what he argued two chapters ago"

→ a close-reading note that becomes a critical review.

The story to tell: the article was already in your margins. Readr just assembles it, in reading order, with your quotes and your voice.

The anti-slop angle

Composed articles must not read like AI wrote them — this audience will smell it instantly and it would poison the brand. Plan: bake a no-ai-slop-style editing pass into the compose pipeline (ban "It's not X, it's Y", colon reveals, puffery, weasel attribution; prefer the reader's own phrasing from their notes; keep their quotes verbatim).

This is also a marketing claim in itself: "Your article, in your voice — the AI arranges, you author." Positioning the human as author (their highlights, their notes, their edits) is both honest and disarms the "AI wrote your review" criticism.

4 · Supporting messages

  • Private by design. Your books stay on your device; local-model mode never touches the network; keys live in the Keychain. For this audience, privacy is a first-class reason to trust the AI features at all.
  • No lock-in. Highlights export as clean Markdown. Your marginalia are yours. (Apple Books' most-cited complaint — no annotation export — is free ammunition.)
  • PDFs are first-class. The switcher's wedge vs. Apple Books.
  • Zero merchandising. The home screen resumes your book; it never sells you anything.

5 · Website concept

Vibe

Calm, curious, a space of your own. Paper, ink, generous whitespace, almost no motion except where it demonstrates the product. The site should feel like reading — if the marketing site is loud, the "distraction-free" claim dies on arrival.

Visual system

Carry the app's Marginalia system (docs/DESIGN.md) straight to the web so app and site are one world:

  • Warm paper surfaces (Paper / Sepia / Dark; honor prefers-color-scheme).
  • Serif for book-voice text (Literata / New York style), sans for chrome.
  • Iris #5B57C7 (#938EE9 on dark) reserved for AI moments only — ✦ glyph, ask affordances, streaming answer demos. Never buttons/nav.
  • Muted literary highlight palette (amber, sage, slate, clay) for highlight visuals.
  • The app's colors, light-only (decided 2026-07-24). The site uses the exact palette of the app screenshots — cream paper #F7F3EB, card #FDFAF4, iris #5B57C7, muted amber/sage/slate highlights — and renders light in every viewer theme (color-scheme: light, no dark variant). Rejected: browns/espresso, and any dark/charcoal site theme. Iris is the primary CTA color (the app itself uses iris for send/Done affordances).
  • Paper texture via @paper-design/shaders — open-source shader library, so the exact texture from the reference is directly usable. Use it as a subtle hero-background grain, not wallpaper; static-texture fallback; never cost scroll performance.
  • iPhone views only (decided 2026-07-24). iPad and Mac mockups were built and then cut — they ate too much vertical space. Every mockup is now an iPhone at true device geometry (390pt screen, never shrunk), each showing a full page of real reading: hero (Moby-Dick ch. 96 + Ask sheet), ask-the-book (Frankenstein ch. 5 with the CRISPR-embryo question in a companion panel), margins (Notes + click-to-compose article). Mac/iPad support is stated, not shown — one line under the ask demo, a trust-strip cell ("iPhone, iPad & Mac"), and the closing CTA.
    Copyright line: full pages come from public-domain books only (Moby-Dick, Frankenstein); in-copyright titles appear as short highlighted excerpts with attribution.
  • Paper texture follows the shaders.paper.design model — three layers over the hero photo: folds/crumples (embossed via SVG diffuse lighting), fiber (stretched turbulence), roughness (fine grain). Photo opacity stays high; only a soft radial paper glow sits behind the headline.
  • One real photo: the hero backdrop. The hero is a paper-treated version of the reader-under-the-willow reference photo (grain + sepia pull + paper scrim). No secondary photo sections (the park-writer band was cut, 2026-07-24). No AI-generated imagery anywhere — one of the reference images had AI tells (garbled cover text); that failure on our site would undercut the anti-slop positioning.
  • All product examples are iOS app screenshots or mockups that pass for them (decided 2026-07-24). Hero: iPhone showing the reader + Ask sheet. Example bank: cropped app-screen cards (book title in the nav, real page text with a highlight, question, ✦ answer). Margins: iPhone showing the Notes panel.
  • Real passages from real books. Anusree supplied photographed pages from her own copies (2026-07-24); the site now quotes them exactly, in short highlighted fragments with attribution:
    • Why Has Nobody Told Me This Before? (Dr Julie Smith) — "Rumination is like a thoughts washing machine." → "Is there real evidence behind this?"
    • The Gene (Siddhartha Mukherjee) — reverse transcriptase, Baltimore & Temin 1970 → "Is this the enzyme mRNA vaccines use?" (a beyond-the-book answer, demonstrating the decided behavior)
    • Project Hail Mary (Andy Weir) — astrophage/spin drive → "Could astrophage really exist?"
    • Shoe Dog (Phil Knight) — powers the compose demo: three highlights from p. 84 become the article "Something I could point to". Chosen deliberately for a Product Hunt audience of founders.
    Same four books fill the library-grid mini. Keep excerpts short and attributed.

Page structure — single page + a couple of leaves

  1. Hero — paper-textured, quiet. Tagline + one line + one CTA. Behind it, a live-feeling demo: a serif paragraph, a sentence gets selected, a marginal question appears ("Is this true?"), an answer streams in Iris.
  2. The rabbithole section — rotating example bank. Copy: "Every question you'd normally lose a browser tab to."
  3. Margins → article — a highlight + scribbled note morphing into a composed article. Copy: "The article was already in your margins."
  4. Trust strip — private by design / your model or a local one / Markdown export / no store, no ads.
  5. Footer CTA — download (macOS) or waitlist (iOS/TestFlight), pending launch-scope decision.
  6. Leaves — a plain-English privacy page only. Manifesto page cut (2026-07-24): "no one has time to read all that."
  7. "The reader" section (added 2026-07-24, right after the hero) — three app-screen minis that carry the base value prop before the AI story: Library (EPUBs/PDFs, no store), Appearance (serif type, paper themes), Choose your model (Claude / ChatGPT / local, Keychain note). The compose demo is interactive: tapping ✦ Compose article reveals the composed article.
  8. Hero photo — golden-hour reader by a tree, by Ioana Ye on Unsplash (Unsplash license, no attribution required), paper-treated and embedded as a data URI in the page.

Copy rules

  • Run all site copy through the no-ai-slop rules; the site must pass its own product's bar.
  • Short sentences. Book-review register, not SaaS register. No "unleash," "supercharge," "revolutionize."
  • Examples over adjectives: show the mid-century-England question rather than saying "powerful contextual answers."

6 · Open questions — all answered (2026-07-23)

  1. Website's job — decided: Product Hunt launch + waitlist. The site is a pre-launch page: waitlist CTA, "launching on Product Hunt" framing, copy in near-future tense. Build the PH launch assets (gallery images, maker comment, first-comment FAQ) from the same collateral.
  2. Name — shortlist below; decide before domains/wordmark.
  3. AI-forwardness — decided: reading-first, but name the AI plainly. Don't hide it — the AI is how Readr differs from every other reader, so the hero and trust strip say it outright ("AI that has read the whole book", "your model or a local one"). What we still avoid: AI as identity, hype register, ✦ everywhere. AI is named where it explains the difference, quiet everywhere else.
  4. Beyond-the-book question behavior — resolved: book-contextual, not book-limited (see Section 2); implementation gap tracked in issue #54.
  5. Where do shared articles live — decided: copy + export as Markdown/PDF is enough for now. No hosted/social layer; drop "post" language from site copy in favor of "share or keep".

Naming shortlist

The name-space around "reader" is crowded (Readwise Reader, Readest…). Candidates, in rough order of my preference — all need domain + trademark checks before committing:

  • Commonplace — after commonplace books, the centuries-old practice of copying passages into a notebook alongside your own thoughts. That is literally the highlights→article feature. Calm, literary, and it gives the article feature a beautiful story ("your commonplace book, composed").
  • Dogear — the fold you leave so you can come back. Short, warm, informal, very ownable as a wordmark; skews less scholarly than Commonplace.
  • Marginalia — already the internal design-system name; the product genuinely lives in the margins. Slightly academic in register.
  • Flyleaf — the blank page at the front of every book; "your space in the book." Pretty, slightly obscure.
  • Footnote — matches the site's own sign-off ("made for people who read the footnotes"); crowded namespace.
  • Burrow — the rabbithole made cozy; least book-coded of the list.
  • Readr (keep) — descriptive and safe, but hard to own or search for.

7 · Deliberately not doing (for now)

  • Comparison/versus pages (Apple Books, Kindle) — ammunition exists, but leading with a fight undercuts the calm brand. Revisit post-launch.
  • Feature-grid marketing ("50+ features!") — antithetical to the vibe.
  • AI-generated imagery — same poison as slop text for this audience. Real screenshots, real texture, real type.
